What does an image generator draw when you ask for a Democrat?

I asked one image generator for a person who is “a Democrat”, then for a person who is “a Republican”, and changed nothing else. Every person below is synthetic. None of them is a real Democrat, a real Republican, or a photograph of anyone.

a Democrat

What changed between the walls

One word. Every portrait on the second wall has a partner on the first: the same random seed, the same stated group and gender, the same setting words. Only “Democrat” became “Republican”.

That is why the walls look alike. A seed fixes most of the layout, so the question is what the label moves, and by how much. I also ran two non-political labels and a no-label version, so a political difference can be judged against the difference any swapped label makes.

Nothing here was picked for effect: each wall shows every image for that label. Click any portrait to see all versions of the same seed.

Why I made this

Motivation, audience and the Responsible AI need.

Image generators now draw the pictures that go into slides, posters, news graphics and social posts. When someone types “a Democrat” or “a Republican”, the model supplies everything the words leave out: who the person is, how old, what they wear, where they stand. Those defaults are the model’s own idea of the label.

People already carry inaccurate pictures of the two parties, as the next section shows. I wanted to know whether a generator carries a picture too, and in particular whether it ties a party to a race, a gender or an age, the way audits of other labels have found that image models tie them to particular looks.

This is for three audiences: people who use these tools and may not notice what the defaults add, students and teachers who want an audit they can read without a machine-learning background, and researchers who want a protocol they can rerun on another model. Anyone a label gets applied to is affected, because a default picture could shape what viewers expect a typical member to look like.

I did not ask what real Democrats or Republicans look like, and no real person’s photograph is used. The pictures are the model’s output and nothing more.

Why would a model draw a party?

Three statements, each marked as established or open.

Established, about people

People carry a picture of partisans in their heads

In a national survey of 1,000 Americans, people guessed that about a third of Democrats are gay, lesbian or bisexual (about 6% are) and that nearly four in ten Republicans earn over $250,000 a year (about 2% do). Another set of surveys asked people for the words that come to mind for each party’s supporters: Democrats were “liberal”, “caring” and “minorities”; Republicans were “conservative”, “rich” and “white”. People who think of partisans mostly in terms of traits are more polarized.

Ahler & Sood 2018; Rothschild et al. 2018. Both are surveys of people, not of AI.

Established, about related models

Image models trained on web pictures pick up human-like associations

Researchers found human-like biases in image representations learned from web photos, and found that Stable Diffusion produces stereotyped people from ordinary prompts, including prompts with no mention of race or gender. Adding wording to counter the stereotype often did not remove the pattern.

Steed & Caliskan 2021; Bianchi et al. 2023. Neither tested political identity.

Open, and what this project asks

Does one changed word move the picture?

None of the four papers above tests whether a text-to-image model draws a person differently when only the party label changes. Closer work exists: a 2025 preprint prompted Stable Diffusion 1.5 and Flux-1 with “a photo of a democratic person” and “a photo of a republican person” among many ideology prompts, and labeled perceived race, gender, age and body type. In its Stable Diffusion 1.5 table, the democratic prompt drew a more racially mixed set than the republican one (64% White and 26% Black, against 100% White). It did not pair seeds, add non-political comparison labels or report uncertainty, and it gave the party contrast only brief remarks. This audit asks that narrower question with those controls, for one model and one prompt setup. Any difference is an association in one model’s output. It does not show why the model does it, and it says nothing about what real partisans look like.

Porikli & Porikli, arXiv:2506.13780.

How it was run

The model is an image generator, run locally. For each seed, stated group and stated gender, the same prompt was generated five times, once per label: a Democrat, a Republican, a Rotary Club member, a neighborhood association member, and no label. The two non-political labels are the yardstick: they show how much any swapped group label changes a portrait.

The run The prompt states group and gender in words; what a portrait looks like is a separate matter and is never treated as a fact about the person.

A local vision-language model read each portrait using a written codebook, seeing only the image. One person labeled 90 portraits blind to check it, and a feature only enters the main tests if the model agrees with those labels well enough. Differences are risk differences averaged over seeds, with intervals from resampling seeds and a permutation test on Democrat minus Republican, corrected across the main features that passed that check.

The plan, every change to it, and the codebook are in the repository. They are a dated analysis plan with a change log, not a time-stamped pre-registration.

This is a black-box audit with controlled interventions, not an explanation of the model’s insides. The course ideas it uses:

  • A counterfactual comparison: the same seed, group and gender, with only the label changed.
  • Non-political labels as a yardstick, so a difference can be judged against a difference that means nothing politically.
  • A written codebook of observable features, and a check of the automated labels against my own blind labels.
  • Uncertainty from resampling seeds, a permutation test, a correction for several tests, and a replication whose prediction was written first.
  • A dated plan with a change log, so analyses added after seeing results are marked as exploratory.
  • An exhibit built for people outside the course, showing every image.

Not used, on purpose: feature-attribution tools such as SHAP or LIME, attention maps, and probing of the network. They would answer why the model behaves this way, not what it draws when one word changes. The README lists every concept and where it lives in the code.

What this cannot tell you

  • These people are synthetic. They are not samples of real Democrats or Republicans.
  • One model, one prompt wording and one negative prompt. Another model, or another phrasing, may behave differently.
  • A seed fixes most of a picture, so only large differences can be detected with this number of seeds. “No difference found” is not “no bias”.
  • A difference would show an association in the output. It would not show why, and training data is only one of several possible causes.
  • The negative prompt removes illustrations and bare torsos, so the model’s full range is not shown.
  • The stated group word also changes the background, for example market stalls or murals, which is a finding about the model that this audit only touches.
  • Skin tone and age, where they appear, are one person’s impressions of synthetic pictures in three coarse bins, and lighting affects them. They are not anyone’s identity.
  • One annotator checked the automated labels. Agreement shows that two readers align, not that either is right.
  • A difference is not automatically harm, and identical pictures are not automatically fair. What matters depends on who uses the pictures and for what.
